Backblaze explains why it's sharing customer information with advertisers

Summary

Backblaze, the cloud backup provider known for its low-cost, high-volume services, has announced a policy change that will take effect next month. The new terms allow the company to share customer data—including personally identifying information (PII) such as names, email addresses, and potentially device identifiers—with advertising partners. The change is positioned as a revenue‑generating strategy, but it directly conflicts with the expectations of many enterprise customers who rely on Backblaze for secure, private backups.

For enterprise IT teams, this shift raises immediate concerns about data exposure. Backblaze’s customer base includes small and medium businesses that store sensitive files, intellectual property, and regulatory‑protected data. The policy change means that backups stored in Backblaze’s infrastructure could be accessed by third‑party advertisers, creating a new attack surface and a potential breach of confidentiality. Compliance frameworks such as GDPR, UK GDPR, and industry‑specific regulations (e.g., HIPAA, PCI‑DSS) often require strict controls over PII. The new policy could render Backblaze non‑compliant for organizations that must maintain data residency and privacy guarantees.

Vendors and technologies involved include Backblaze itself, the advertising partners that will receive the data, and any third‑party encryption or data‑masking solutions that enterprises may deploy to protect backups. The broader industry implication is a reinforcement of the trend toward data monetization by cloud providers. It signals that even “pure” backup services are not immune to revenue‑driven policy changes. Other vendors may follow suit, prompting a shift toward more privacy‑centric backup solutions or the adoption of on‑prem or hybrid architectures that give enterprises full control over data.

Enterprise IT teams must now reassess their backup strategy, evaluate whether Backblaze remains an acceptable risk, and consider migration or additional safeguards. The incident underscores the importance of continuous monitoring of vendor policy changes and the need for robust data‑classification and encryption practices to mitigate emerging privacy risks.

How to apply

Evaluate

- Inventory all data stored in Backblaze: file types, sensitivity, regulatory classification. - Review current backup policies, retention schedules, and encryption at rest/encryption in transit. - Map data flows to determine if PII is included in backups and whether it is already encrypted with customer‑managed keys. - Conduct a compliance gap analysis against GDPR, UK GDPR, HIPAA, PCI‑DSS, and any internal data‑handling policies.

Propose

- Quantify potential risk exposure: number of records, value of PII, likelihood of breach, and regulatory fines. - Build a cost‑benefit model: compare current backup spend vs. cost of migration to a stricter provider or on‑prem solution. - Include metrics: backup success rate, recovery point objective (RPO), recovery time objective (RTO), and encryption coverage percentage. - Present a phased migration plan with projected downtime and budget impact.

Tools to consider

- Backup vendors: Veeam Backup & Replication, Commvault, Rubrik, Cohesity, Druva (for cloud‑native). - Encryption solutions: Symantec Data Loss Prevention, Thales e-Security, Microsoft Azure Key Vault, AWS KMS, HashiCorp Vault. - Data‑classification platforms: Varonis, Digital Guardian, Symantec Data Loss Prevention. - Migration orchestration: CloudEndure, Zerto, Veeam Cloud Connect.

Risks to flag

- Technical: Loss of data integrity during migration, increased latency, potential for misconfiguration of encryption keys. - Compliance: Non‑compliance with GDPR/UK GDPR if PII is shared; potential fines up to €20M or 4% of global turnover. - Operational: Backup window expansion, staff training requirements, vendor lock‑in with new provider.

Quick win

- Enable client‑side encryption for all Backblaze backups using a customer‑managed key before the policy change takes effect. - Update backup scripts to exclude PII files or move them to a separate, encrypted volume. - Conduct a one‑off audit of backup logs to confirm encryption status and data classification.

Long-term play

- Deploy a hybrid backup architecture: on‑prem or edge storage for highly regulated data, cloud backup for less sensitive workloads. - Implement a data‑classification engine to automatically tag and route data to appropriate storage tiers. - Negotiate a data‑processing agreement with Backblaze that includes strict non‑sharing clauses or transition to a vendor with proven privacy commitments. - Establish a continuous monitoring program for vendor policy changes, integrating alerts into the security operations center (SOC).
