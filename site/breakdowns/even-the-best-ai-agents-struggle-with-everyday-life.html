Even the best AI agents struggle with everyday life

TechCrunch · 24 Nov 2026

Summary

ShellBench, a benchmark developed in partnership with Red Hat, Hugging Face and other industry players, evaluated the performance of leading AI agents from OpenAI, Anthropic and Meta on a set of everyday tasks that mimic real‑world personal workflows. The study found that each agent failed on more than 15 % of the test cases, particularly when dealing with messy personal workspaces such as disorganized calendars, unstructured email threads and spreadsheets with inconsistent formatting. The failures ranged from incorrect data extraction to unsafe actions that could compromise user privacy or violate organizational policies.

For enterprise IT teams, this is a direct signal that the “personal assistant” features being integrated into productivity suites like Google Workspace, Microsoft 365 or custom internal tools are not yet reliable enough for unsupervised deployment. The benchmark highlights gaps in agents’ ability to understand context, maintain state across sessions, and enforce safety constraints—capabilities that are critical when the assistant is expected to schedule meetings, draft emails or manipulate financial data.

Vendors involved include OpenAI (ChatGPT), Anthropic (Claude), Meta (Llama), Red Hat (OpenShift), and Hugging Face (Transformers). The broader industry implication is a shift toward more rigorous testing frameworks for generative AI, a push for standardized safety and reliability metrics, and an increased focus on governance models that can detect and mitigate failures before they reach end users. Regulators may also look more closely at AI‑driven productivity tools, especially in sectors where data sensitivity and compliance are paramount.

Enterprise IT must therefore treat AI assistants as a new class of software that requires continuous monitoring, audit trails, and fallback mechanisms. The ShellBench results underscore the need for robust validation before rolling out AI features, and they point to the necessity of integrating safety checks into the CI/CD pipelines that deliver these assistants to end users.

How to apply

Evaluate

• Inventory all AI‑enabled tools in use (e.g., Google Workspace AI, Microsoft 365 Copilot, custom OpenAI integrations). • Map out the data flows that the agents touch: calendars, email, spreadsheets, CRM, and any proprietary datasets. • Run a pilot ShellBench‑style test or equivalent in a sandbox to quantify failure rates on your own data. • Review existing audit logs for unsupervised agent activity and identify any incidents of policy violation or data leakage.

Propose

• Present a risk‑benefit matrix: quantify potential productivity gains versus the probability of unsafe actions (e.g., mis‑scheduled meetings, accidental data exposure). • Use metrics such as “failure rate per 1,000 tasks” and “time to remediation” from your pilot tests. • Recommend a phased rollout with mandatory human oversight for the first 90 days, backed by a SLA that guarantees rollback within 24 h if safety thresholds are breached. • Highlight cost savings from reduced support tickets once the agent’s reliability improves.

Tools to consider

• Red Hat OpenShift for containerizing and monitoring AI workloads. • Hugging Face Model Hub for deploying vetted models with built‑in safety layers. • Microsoft 365 Compliance Center and Google Workspace Security Center for policy enforcement. • Datadog or Prometheus for real‑time telemetry on agent actions. • OpenAI’s API with fine‑tuning and safety guardrails, or Anthropic’s Claude with the “Safety” endpoint.

Risks to flag

• Technical: data corruption, unintended API calls, lack of state persistence. • Compliance: UK GDPR violations through unauthorized data sharing, lack of audit trails. • Operational: escalation delays, over‑reliance on AI leading to skill erosion, vendor lock‑in.

Quick win

• Deploy a lightweight monitoring script that logs every agent command, flags any that modify calendar or email without explicit user confirmation, and sends alerts to a dedicated Slack channel. • Within 30 days, you’ll have visibility into unsafe actions and can block or quarantine problematic agents.

Long-term play

LONG‑TERM PLAY • Over 6–12 months, build a governance framework that includes: – A formal AI risk register with defined thresholds. – Continuous integration of updated ShellBench or similar benchmarks into the QA pipeline. – Training for developers on safe prompt engineering and model fine‑tuning. – Collaboration with vendors to adopt shared safety metrics and open‑source safety modules. • Aim to achieve a 90 %+ success rate on everyday tasks before allowing full‑scale, unsupervised deployment.
