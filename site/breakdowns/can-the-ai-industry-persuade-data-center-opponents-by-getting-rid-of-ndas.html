Can the AI industry persuade data center opponents by getting rid of NDAs?

TechCrunch · 11 Oct 202 · Generated 11 Oct 2026, 21:48

Summary

TechCrunch reports that a coalition of AI companies is exploring the removal of non‑disclosure agreements (NDAs) as a strategy to mitigate opposition from data center operators and regulators. The proposal stems from growing public and political scrutiny over the energy consumption, carbon footprint, and land use of large AI training facilities. By eliminating NDAs, the industry argues it can demonstrate transparency in power usage, cooling efficiency, and supply chain sourcing, potentially easing licensing hurdles and community resistance.

For enterprise IT teams, this development signals a shift in how data center projects may be evaluated by local governments and environmental agencies. If the AI sector succeeds in making its operations public, regulators may adopt similar transparency mandates for all large‑scale compute deployments. This could require enterprises to disclose detailed metrics on power usage effectiveness (PUE), renewable energy sourcing, and carbon emissions, and to provide real‑time monitoring dashboards.

Key vendors and technologies implicated include major cloud providers (AWS, Azure, GCP) that host AI workloads, as well as specialized hardware suppliers such as NVIDIA, AMD, and Intel. Software platforms for energy monitoring—such as Schneider Electric’s EcoStruxure, Siemens’ MindSphere, and open‑source solutions like OpenBMC—will become more critical. Additionally, compliance tools that map to UK GDPR and other data protection frameworks will need to integrate with these transparency workflows.

Industry implications are significant. A move toward mandatory disclosure could accelerate the adoption of green data center designs, incentivize the use of renewable energy credits, and create a competitive advantage for providers that can prove lower emissions. Conversely, firms that fail to adapt may face stricter permitting processes, higher compliance costs, and reputational damage. The policy debate may also influence the licensing of new data center sites, potentially tightening zoning regulations and increasing the cost of capital for large compute projects.

Enterprise IT leaders must anticipate that future regulatory frameworks may require detailed reporting on energy use, supply chain provenance, and environmental impact. Preparing for these changes now—by auditing current data center footprints, establishing robust monitoring, and aligning with emerging compliance standards—will reduce exposure to regulatory risk and position the organization to capitalize on any new transparency‑driven market opportunities.
