Detailed summary

Apple’s macOS includes two undocumented background services—knowledgeconstructiond and knowledgegraphd—that automatically aggregate data from a wide range of user sources: Contacts, Calendar, Photos, Messages, Mail, Location, and Screen Time. The services build a personal knowledge graph that maps relationships between users and their contacts, effectively creating a detailed social network of every Mac user. A newly released open‑source tool called iKnow allows administrators to inspect the graph, revealing the extent of data collection. The discovery was highlighted on Hacker News and has raised immediate concerns for enterprise IT teams that manage fleets of macOS devices.

For enterprises, the issue matters because these daemons operate without user consent or clear policy controls, potentially exposing sensitive employee relationships, communication patterns, and location data. If left unchecked, the knowledge graph could be accessed by internal actors, third‑party apps, or even malicious actors if the services are compromised. The services are not documented in Apple’s official security or privacy documentation, which means standard MDM or endpoint protection tools may not automatically detect or control them.

Vendors and technologies involved include Apple’s macOS (specifically the undocumented daemons), mobile device management (MDM) solutions such as Jamf, Microsoft Intune, and VMware Workspace ONE, and endpoint security platforms that can monitor background processes. The broader industry implication is a renewed focus on “hidden” data collection mechanisms in consumer operating systems and the need for stricter audit and control frameworks in enterprise environments. Regulators may also scrutinize such practices under privacy laws like UK GDPR, where data minimization and transparency are mandatory.

The incident underscores the importance of proactive discovery, policy enforcement, and monitoring of all system daemons, even those not publicly documented. Failure to do so could lead to unintentional data exposure, compliance violations, and erosion of employee trust.

IT engineer application guide

Evaluate

- Scan all macOS devices for the presence of knowledgeconstructiond and knowledgegraphd using MDM inventory or command‑line tools (e.g., launchctl list | grep -E 'knowledge.*'). - Verify which user accounts are running the daemons and the scope of data sources they access. - Assess current MDM policy templates to determine if they include restrictions on undocumented services or background data collection. - Review audit logs for any outbound network activity originating from these daemons.

Propose

- Quantify the potential data exposure: estimate the number of employee contacts, messages, and location points that could be aggregated per device. - Benchmark against compliance requirements: UK GDPR mandates data minimization; calculate the percentage of personal data that would be collected without explicit consent. - Present a risk matrix comparing the likelihood of unauthorized access versus the impact on employee privacy and corporate reputation. - Recommend a phased approach: immediate audit, short‑term disabling, long‑term policy integration.

Tools to consider

- Jamf Pro: MDM policy to disable or restrict launchd services. - Microsoft Intune: Device configuration profiles for macOS to block background daemons. - VMware Workspace ONE: Endpoint security and process monitoring. - Sysinternals Process Explorer (macOS port) or native launchctl for manual inspection. - iKnow (open‑source) for graph visualization and validation.

Risks to flag

- Technical: Disabling daemons may affect legitimate Apple services that rely on them; risk of system instability. - Compliance: Failure to document controls could violate UK GDPR Article 6 (lawful basis) and Article 5 (data minimization). - Operational: Users may experience reduced functionality in apps that depend on the knowledge graph (e.g., Siri, Spotlight). - Security: Unmonitored daemons could become a vector for data exfiltration if compromised.

Quick win

- Deploy a one‑off MDM script that stops and unloads knowledgeconstructiond and knowledgegraphd on all managed Macs. - Verify the change via a quick inventory report and confirm no outbound traffic from these services. - Communicate the change to end users with a brief notice explaining the privacy improvement.

Long-term play

- Integrate daemon control into the baseline MDM policy and enforce it through continuous compliance monitoring. - Implement a regular audit cadence (quarterly) to detect any re‑appearance of undocumented services. - Develop an internal policy document that defines acceptable background data collection, including a clear exemption list for Apple‑approved services. - Coordinate with Apple through the Enterprise Support Program to request official documentation or a formal deprecation of these daemons. - Conduct a privacy impact assessment (PIA) to ensure all data flows comply with UK GDPR and other relevant regulations.
