Hackers obtain counterfeit TLS certificates for Google and other large services

Ars Technica · 06 Oct 202 · Generated 27 Oct 2026, 12:00

Read the original →

Summary

Hackers recently exploited administrative control over the .gh (Ghana), .sl (Sierra Leone), and .as (American Samoa) country code top‑level domains to generate counterfeit TLS certificates for high‑profile brands, including Google. The attackers leveraged the ability to publish DNS‑based Certificate Authority Authorization (CAA) records that were misconfigured or absent, allowing them to issue certificates that passed validation by browsers and certificate transparency (CT) logs. Google has responded by blocking the forged certificates in Chrome and has issued a public advisory urging all domain owners to audit their CAA records and CT logs. The incident highlights the critical role of domain registrar configuration, DNS security, and CT logging in the trust chain that underpins secure web traffic.

For enterprise IT teams, the attack underscores several immediate concerns. First, any domain that is part of an organization’s SSO, MDM, or internal services could be targeted if its CAA records are not strictly scoped to approved CAs. Second, the use of CT logs as a monitoring mechanism is now a frontline defense; unexpected entries in a log can indicate a compromise before browsers detect it. Third, the incident demonstrates that even large vendors can be vulnerable to third‑party domain hijacking, so internal controls must be robust regardless of external brand reputation.

Vendors and technologies involved include domain registrars (e.g., GoDaddy, Namecheap, Cloudflare), DNS providers, CT log operators (e.g., Google Certificate Transparency, DigiCert), and browser vendors (Chrome, Firefox, Edge). The broader industry implication is a renewed focus on DNS‑based security controls, such as mandatory CAA records, DNSSEC, and continuous CT log monitoring. Regulatory bodies may also tighten requirements for certificate issuance and domain ownership verification, especially under frameworks like GDPR where data integrity and security are core principles.

Enterprise IT must therefore treat domain configuration as a security asset, enforce strict CAA policies, and integrate CT log alerts into security operations. Failure to do so exposes users to man‑in‑the‑middle attacks that can compromise authentication tokens, VPN connections, and other critical services. The incident serves as a wake‑up call that the trust chain is only as strong as its weakest link, and that proactive domain hygiene is essential for maintaining enterprise security posture.

How to apply

Evaluate

- Inventory all domains and subdomains used by the organization, including third‑party services and internal tooling. - Verify that each domain has a CAA record that explicitly lists only the CAs approved by the organization (e.g., Google, DigiCert, Let's Encrypt if used). - Export and review all CT log entries for the organization’s domains over the past 30 days; flag any certificates that were not issued by an approved CA. - Check DNSSEC status for each domain; ensure it is enabled and correctly signed. - Confirm that the domain registrar’s API access is restricted to authorized personnel and that audit logs are enabled.

Propose

- Quantify risk: estimate the potential impact of a compromised TLS certificate on SSO, MDM, and VPN services (e.g., downtime, data exfiltration, credential theft). - Benchmark: compare current CAA compliance rate against industry best practice (target 100% compliance). - Cost estimate: allocate budget for DNSSEC deployment, CT log monitoring tools, and staff training (e.g., $5,000–$10,000 for tooling, $2,000 for training). - ROI: demonstrate that preventing even a single certificate compromise can save the organization from costly incident response, regulatory fines, and brand damage.

Tools to consider

- DNSSEC management: Infoblox, Cloudflare DNS, AWS Route 53. - CAA record audit: DNSViz, CAA‑Check, or custom scripts using dnspython. - CT log monitoring: Google Certificate Transparency API, DigiCert Certificate Transparency Log Viewer, or commercial solutions like Qualys SSL Labs or Rapid7 InsightVM. - Alerting: Splunk, Elastic Stack, or native SIEM integration with CT log feeds. - Policy enforcement: Terraform or Pulumi scripts to enforce CAA records as code.

Risks to flag

- Technical: Misconfigured CAA records can inadvertently block legitimate certificate issuance, causing service outages. - Compliance: Under UK GDPR, failure to protect data integrity and confidentiality can lead to fines up to 4% of global turnover. - Operational: Over‑restrictive CAA policies may delay emergency certificate renewals during incidents. - Vendor lock‑in: Relying on a single registrar or DNS provider may limit flexibility.

Quick win

- Within 30 days, run a full CAA audit, correct any misconfigurations, and enable DNSSEC on all domains. Deploy a simple CT log monitoring script that sends alerts to the security ops channel whenever a new certificate is issued for an organization domain.

Long-term play

- Over 6–12 months, implement a policy‑as‑code framework that automatically generates and validates CAA records during domain provisioning. Integrate CT log monitoring into the CI/CD pipeline for certificate issuance. Conduct quarterly penetration tests focused on DNS and certificate issuance paths. Establish a formal incident response playbook that includes steps for rapid revocation of compromised certificates and communication with browser vendors.
