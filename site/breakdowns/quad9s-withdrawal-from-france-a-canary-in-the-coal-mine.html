Quad9's withdrawal from France: A canary in the coal mine

Hacker News · 17 Nov 202 · Generated 18 Nov 2026, 00:51

Summary

Quad9, a well‑known free public DNS resolver, has announced that it will no longer serve DNS queries from France following a court order that demanded the blocking of sites alleged to host copyrighted content. The French court imposed a €200,000 fine on Quad9 for not complying with the request, leading the company to withdraw its service from the country. This action is significant for enterprise IT teams because it illustrates how DNS providers can become targets of national legal actions, potentially disrupting the DNS resolution path that many organizations rely on for both internal and external traffic. Enterprises that currently use Quad9 or similar public resolvers (e.g., Cloudflare 1.1.1.1, Google Public DNS, OpenDNS) must now consider the stability of those services in the face of legal pressure. The incident also raises questions about the adequacy of DNS security controls, such as DNSSEC validation, and the need for internal DNS infrastructure that can be hardened against censorship or shutdowns. From a vendor perspective, the event highlights the importance of understanding the legal frameworks that affect DNS operators. Cloudflare, which has previously faced similar scrutiny, may adjust its compliance posture, while other providers may adopt stricter filtering or offer enterprise‑grade services with guaranteed uptime. The broader industry implication is a potential shift toward more localized or self‑hosted DNS solutions, increased scrutiny of DNS traffic logs, and a renewed focus on DNS resilience as part of overall cyber‑resilience strategies. Organizations should monitor regulatory developments in the EU and other jurisdictions, evaluate the risk of DNS outages, and plan for rapid migration to alternative resolvers or internal deployments to maintain uninterrupted name resolution for critical services.

How to apply

Evaluate

- Inventory all DNS resolvers in use (public, cloud, on‑prem). - Map traffic flows: internal hosts, external services, VPN endpoints. - Review SLA guarantees and historical uptime of each resolver. - Check compliance with data residency and privacy laws (GDPR, UK GDPR). - Identify any existing DNS filtering or blocking policies that could trigger legal action.

Propose

- Quantify potential downtime impact: calculate average DNS query volume, critical service dependencies, and mean time to recovery. - Build a cost‑benefit model: compare current public resolver costs (free) versus internal resolver deployment (hardware, software, staffing). - Include metrics: 99.99% availability target, mean time to detect (MTTD) DNS failure, and mean time to recover (MTTR). - Present risk mitigation ROI: reduced exposure to legal fines, improved compliance posture, and enhanced control over DNS data.

Tools to consider

- BIND9 or NSD for internal authoritative and recursive resolvers. - Unbound or Knot Resolver for high‑performance recursive caching. - DNS over HTTPS (DoH) or DNS over TLS (DoT) support for secure outbound queries. - Cloudflare Enterprise DNS, Google Cloud DNS, or Amazon Route 53 Resolver for managed, compliant options. - Monitoring solutions: Prometheus with node_exporter, Grafana dashboards, or commercial tools like SolarWinds DNS Monitor.

Risks to flag

- Technical: misconfiguration leading to split‑brain DNS, cache poisoning, or loss of DNSSEC validation. - Compliance: storing DNS query logs in jurisdictions with stricter privacy laws; potential GDPR violations if logs are not anonymized. - Operational: staff skill gaps, increased maintenance overhead, and potential service disruption during migration. - Legal: inadvertent blocking of legitimate sites, leading to regulatory scrutiny or customer impact.

Quick win

- Deploy a secondary recursive resolver (e.g., Unbound) in a redundant data center. - Configure local hosts to use the internal resolver first, with a fallback to the public resolver. - Implement basic health checks and alerting within 7 days. - Document the new DNS path for incident response teams.

Long-term play

- Over 6–12 months, transition to a fully internal DNS stack: authoritative zones for all internal domains, recursive caching with DNSSEC validation, and DoH/DoT outbound. - Integrate with existing identity and access management (IAM) for fine‑grained DNS filtering. - Establish a DNS governance framework: change management, audit logs, and compliance reporting. - Evaluate hybrid models: combine internal resolvers with enterprise‑grade public services for redundancy, ensuring automatic failover and load balancing. - Conduct regular penetration testing and compliance audits to validate resilience against legal and technical threats.
