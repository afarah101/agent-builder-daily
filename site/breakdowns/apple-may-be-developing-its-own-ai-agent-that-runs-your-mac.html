Apple may be developing its own AI agent that runs your Mac

Detailed summary

Apple is reportedly developing an AI agent that can autonomously use macOS applications, browse the web, and execute code, effectively creating a built‑in computer assistant for Mac devices. The new agent will run with elevated privileges, allowing it to interact with system services, access user data, and perform tasks that previously required explicit user interaction. Apple is simultaneously tightening its security model by adding new controls that prevent third‑party agents from gaining full system access. These controls will surface in macOS 27.3 as warning prompts when an application requests full agent access, and they will be enforced by the operating system’s sandboxing and app‑signing mechanisms.

For enterprise IT teams, this development means a shift in how Mac endpoints are managed. Existing Mobile Device Management (MDM) and endpoint protection solutions must adapt to the new privilege model, ensuring that corporate policies can still enforce least‑privilege access while accommodating the legitimate use of the native AI agent. Failure to update MDM profiles could leave Macs vulnerable to unverified AI agents that might bypass existing security layers, potentially exposing sensitive data or enabling lateral movement within the network. The new controls also impact how third‑party automation tools, such as scripting frameworks, automation platforms, and custom AI assistants, are deployed and monitored.

Vendors and technologies involved include Apple’s macOS 27.3, the Apple Business Manager (ABM) and Apple School Manager (ASM) for device enrollment, and third‑party MDM solutions such as Jamf, Microsoft Intune, VMware Workspace ONE, and Cisco Meraki. Endpoint protection platforms like CrowdStrike Falcon, SentinelOne, and Sophos Intercept X will need to update their detection rules to recognize the new agent’s behavior and differentiate it from malicious actors. Cloud‑based AI services that integrate with macOS, such as OpenAI or Anthropic APIs, may also be affected by the new sandboxing restrictions.

The broader industry implications are significant. As operating systems move toward integrated AI assistants, the boundary between user‑initiated and autonomous actions blurs, raising new security and privacy concerns. Enterprises will need to rethink their security posture, adopting more granular access controls, continuous monitoring, and AI‑aware threat detection. The shift also signals a trend toward platform‑centric AI, where vendors like Apple, Microsoft, and Google will embed AI capabilities directly into their OSes, potentially reducing the market for third‑party AI assistants but increasing the need for robust policy management and compliance frameworks.
