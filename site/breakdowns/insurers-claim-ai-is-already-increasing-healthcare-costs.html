Insurers claim AI is already increasing healthcare costs

Summary

Blue Cross Blue Shield (BCBS) released a study indicating that artificial intelligence (AI) tools deployed in hospitals added $942 million to healthcare spending over a two‑year period. The analysis, reported by TechCrunch, examined claims data from 2020‑2021 and found that AI‑enabled diagnostics, predictive analytics, and clinical decision support systems were associated with higher utilization and cost per episode than comparable non‑AI workflows. The study does not isolate specific vendors but references common AI platforms such as IBM Watson Health, Google Health, and Microsoft Azure AI, as well as proprietary solutions from large health systems. For enterprise IT teams, the findings raise immediate questions about return on investment (ROI) for AI initiatives, especially when budgets are already stretched by legacy system maintenance and cybersecurity demands. The data suggest that AI can unintentionally drive over‑testing, unnecessary procedures, or higher drug utilization, thereby inflating costs. This has implications for IT governance, cost‑management frameworks, and vendor selection processes. If AI is not delivering measurable savings, it may be a candidate for re‑prioritization or tighter oversight. The broader industry must confront the tension between innovation and cost containment, potentially leading to stricter regulatory scrutiny and a shift toward value‑based AI contracts that tie performance to actual savings. IT leaders should therefore audit current AI deployments, quantify cost impact, and align AI projects with clear financial metrics before scaling further.

How to apply

Evaluate

- Inventory all AI‑enabled applications (clinical decision support, imaging analytics, patient monitoring). - Capture baseline cost metrics: per‑patient spend, utilization rates, and claim amounts before AI implementation. - Map data flows to identify where AI adds value versus where it duplicates existing processes. - Conduct a cost‑benefit analysis using the BCBS study as a benchmark: compare incremental spend to projected savings or efficiency gains.

Propose

- Build a business case that includes: projected cost avoidance (e.g., reduced unnecessary imaging), productivity gains (e.g., faster triage), and risk mitigation (e.g., error reduction). - Use metrics such as cost per diagnosis, average length of stay, and readmission rates. - Benchmark against industry averages (e.g., CMS cost‑effectiveness studies) and internal historical data. - Present a phased ROI model: short‑term (0‑12 months) and long‑term (12‑24 months) with sensitivity analysis.

Tools to consider

- Microsoft Azure AI + Health Bot for scalable, compliant AI services. - Google Cloud Healthcare API integrated with Vertex AI for imaging analytics. - IBM Watson Health for clinical decision support with audit trails. - Evidently AI for predictive analytics with built‑in explainability. - Open‑source frameworks (e.g., MONAI, PyTorch) if in‑house expertise exists.

Risks to flag

- Technical: model drift, data quality degradation, integration latency. - Compliance: UK GDPR and HIPAA – ensure data residency, consent, and auditability. - Operational: over‑reliance on AI leading to alert fatigue, potential for increased utilization. - Vendor lock‑in: evaluate exit strategies and data portability.

Quick win

- Conduct a 30‑day audit of AI‑driven imaging orders: identify orders with no clinical indication and flag for review. - Implement a dashboard that tracks AI‑generated alerts versus actual clinical actions to surface inefficiencies.

Long-term play

- Deploy a governance framework that ties AI model performance to financial KPIs; require quarterly reviews. - Negotiate value‑based contracts with vendors where fees are linked to demonstrable cost savings. - Invest in explainable AI and continuous monitoring to maintain clinical trust and regulatory compliance over 6‑12 months.
