Your TV can see through walls

Summary

A researcher from a university laboratory recently demonstrated that a low‑cost, off‑the‑shelf radio receiver can detect human motion and estimate speed by monitoring selective fading patterns in over‑the‑air TV broadcast signals. The experiment used a simple software‑defined radio (SDR) tuned to a local TV channel and a custom algorithm that tracks rapid amplitude fluctuations caused by a moving body. By correlating these fluctuations with known channel characteristics, the system can infer both the presence and velocity of a person behind a wall or in a blind spot. The same principle applies to other ambient radio bands such as Wi‑Fi, LTE, and 5G, where multipath propagation and Doppler shifts are already exploited for positioning and channel state information.

For enterprise IT teams, this proof‑of‑concept signals a new class of passive sensing solutions that can augment or replace traditional camera‑based intrusion detection. Passive radio sensing requires no line‑of‑sight, can operate in low‑light or smoke conditions, and is less intrusive from a privacy standpoint because it does not capture visual imagery. Vendors such as Aruba Networks, Cisco Meraki, and Ubiquiti have already begun integrating radio‑based motion detection into their access‑point firmware for occupancy sensing. Emerging startups like Motus Networks and Airborne are developing dedicated SDR‑based security sensors that can be deployed on existing Wi‑Fi infrastructure.

The broader industry implication is a shift toward “ambient intelligence” where existing wireless networks double as security sensors. This can reduce the number of cameras needed, lower bandwidth usage for video streams, and provide continuous coverage in areas that are difficult to monitor visually (e.g., stairwells, storage rooms, or outdoor perimeters). However, it also raises new security and privacy concerns: attackers could potentially spoof motion signals or use the same passive sensing to infer employee movements. Regulatory bodies such as the UK GDPR will scrutinize the collection of movement data, even if it is not visual, and enterprises must ensure lawful bases and data minimisation.

Overall, the demonstration highlights that passive radio sensing is moving from academic curiosity to a viable enterprise security tool. IT teams should start evaluating the feasibility of integrating such sensors into their existing wireless infrastructure, assess compliance implications, and monitor vendor roadmaps for commercial offerings.

How to apply

Evaluate

- Inventory all Wi‑Fi, LTE, and 5G access points and their firmware versions. - Map coverage gaps where cameras are ineffective (e.g., high ceilings, underground areas). - Assess current motion‑sensing capabilities (e.g., PIR sensors, camera analytics) and their limitations. - Review data‑collection policies under UK GDPR: does your organization currently log motion or occupancy data? Identify lawful bases and retention schedules.

Propose

- Quantify current camera‑related costs: hardware, maintenance, bandwidth, and privacy‑compliance expenses. - Estimate potential savings: reduce camera count by 20‑30% in high‑risk zones, lower video storage by 40%, cut bandwidth usage by 25%. - Benchmark detection latency: passive radio can detect motion within 100 ms versus 500 ms for video analytics. - Build a ROI model: initial capital for SDR modules or upgraded APs, ongoing firmware updates, and staff training versus projected cost savings and risk reduction.

Tools to consider

- Aruba Instant On APs with built‑in occupancy sensing (supports passive motion detection via Wi‑Fi). - Cisco Meraki MR series with “Smart Sensors” firmware that can be configured for motion alerts. - Ubiquiti UniFi APs with custom firmware (e.g., OpenWrt) that can run SDR-based motion detection scripts. - Dedicated SDR platforms: RTL‑SDR dongles, HackRF One, or USRP B200 for prototyping. - Commercial solutions: Motus Networks’ passive sensing platform, Airborne’s Wi‑Fi motion analytics.

Risks to flag

- Technical: false positives from multipath interference, limited range in high‑metal environments, need for calibration per location. - Compliance: GDPR requires explicit consent or legitimate interest for movement data; ensure data minimisation and anonymisation. - Operational: integration with existing SIEM/alerting systems, potential overload of security alerts, staff training on interpreting passive sensor data. - Vendor lock‑in: proprietary firmware may limit future upgrades; evaluate open‑source alternatives.

Quick win

- Deploy a pilot in a single high‑risk area (e.g., server room entrance) using an existing Meraki MR AP with motion‑sensing firmware enabled. - Configure alerts to send to the existing SIEM and test detection latency against a PIR sensor. - Document false‑positive rate over a 30‑day period and present findings to stakeholders.

Long-term play

- Over 6–12 months, roll out passive motion sensing across all critical zones, integrating data into a unified security analytics platform. - Develop automated response playbooks: trigger HVAC adjustments, lock doors, or alert security personnel based on motion patterns. - Conduct periodic privacy impact assessments and update data‑processing agreements to reflect new sensor data. - Explore machine‑learning models that combine passive radio data with existing video analytics for higher accuracy and reduced alert fatigue.
