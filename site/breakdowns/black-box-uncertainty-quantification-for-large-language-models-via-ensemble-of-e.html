Black-Box Uncertainty Quantification for Large Language Models via Ensemble-of-Ensembles

Wang Ma, Debarun Bhattacharjya, Junkyu Lee, Nhan H Pham · Academic Institution · 2026-11-13 · Generated 16 Nov 2026, 15:25

Summary

The study introduces a black‑box uncertainty quantification framework for large language models (LLMs) that decomposes total predictive uncertainty into two distinct components: prompt ambiguity (aleatoric) and model knowledge gaps (epistemic). The authors construct a nested ensemble architecture. The inner ensemble performs random decoding (e.g., temperature, nucleus sampling) to capture stochastic variation in the model’s output. The outer ensemble perturbs the prompt itself (e.g., paraphrasing, synonym substitution, or controlled noise injection) to expose sensitivity to input phrasing. By aggregating across both ensembles, the method estimates a variance attributable to prompt design and a variance attributable to the model’s internal knowledge state. Experiments on small question‑answering benchmarks demonstrate that the black‑box approach matches white‑box baselines that rely on gradient‑based uncertainty estimates, yet it requires only sampling access to the LLM. The authors also show that the decomposition can flag when a high uncertainty score is driven by ambiguous wording rather than a lack of factual knowledge, enabling targeted prompt engineering or knowledge base augmentation.

For enterprise IT, this capability is critical. LLMs are increasingly deployed for customer support, code generation, and compliance‑related queries. Knowing whether a model’s uncertainty stems from poorly crafted prompts or from genuine knowledge gaps allows teams to prioritize engineering effort: refine prompts versus expanding the model’s training data or integrating external knowledge sources. In regulated environments, misattributing uncertainty can lead to incorrect compliance decisions or expose the organization to audit risk. The method’s black‑box nature means it can be applied to any hosted or on‑prem LLM without requiring internal model access, making it suitable for third‑party SaaS offerings.

Real‑world implications include: 1) automated prompt‑optimization pipelines that reduce support ticket volume; 2) risk‑aware decision engines that flag uncertain outputs for human review; 3) compliance monitoring tools that track epistemic uncertainty as a metric for data freshness and model drift; and 4) cost‑effective model selection by quantifying how much additional training data would reduce epistemic variance versus how much prompt engineering can mitigate aleatoric variance. Overall, the study provides a practical, deployable framework that aligns with enterprise governance, cost control, and operational resilience goals.
