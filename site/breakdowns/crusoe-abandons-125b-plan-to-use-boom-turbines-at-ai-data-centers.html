Crusoe abandons $1.25B plan to use Boom turbines at AI data centers

Detailed summary

Crusoe, a major cloud service provider operating data centers in the Pacific Northwest, has dropped its planned investment of $1.25 billion in Boom Supersonic’s stationary turbine power plants. The turbines were intended to supply clean, high‑capacity electricity to Crusoe’s AI‑heavy workloads, leveraging Boom’s proprietary supersonic combustion technology. The decision follows a reassessment of cost, supply chain risk, and regulatory uncertainty surrounding the new technology. For enterprise IT teams, the move signals that regional data centers will continue to rely on conventional grid power or alternative renewable sources, potentially affecting energy costs and carbon footprints for customers. Vendors involved include Boom Supersonic, which is developing the turbines, and the broader renewable energy ecosystem that may fill the gap. The broader industry implication is a cautionary note on high‑profile, high‑risk energy projects for data center operators. Operators may now prioritize proven renewable sources such as solar, wind, or hydro, and may accelerate investments in energy‑efficient cooling and power usage effectiveness (PUE) metrics. The news also underscores the importance of monitoring energy sourcing trends, as shifts in local grid mix or renewable availability can influence cloud consumption costs. Enterprises should anticipate potential price volatility and plan for flexible procurement strategies, including power purchase agreements (PPAs) and on‑site generation options. The decision may prompt other operators to re‑evaluate similar projects, potentially slowing the adoption of novel turbine technologies until they mature and prove cost‑effective at scale.

IT engineer application guide

Evaluate

- Audit current PUE and energy cost per compute unit across all data center sites. - Map regional grid mix and forecast changes in renewable penetration for the next 12–24 months. - Review existing PPAs and renewable credits to identify gaps or opportunities for renegotiation. - Assess the impact of potential energy price spikes on SLA guarantees and cost‑to‑serve metrics.

Propose

- Build a business case comparing the cost of conventional grid power versus the projected cost of Boom turbines, including CAPEX, OPEX, and risk premiums. - Use metrics such as kWh per CPU core, CO₂e per compute, and total cost of ownership (TCO) over a 5‑year horizon. - Benchmark against industry peers who have adopted proven renewable solutions (e.g., wind‑powered data centers in Texas or solar in California). - Highlight potential savings from improved PUE and reduced cooling loads if renewable sources are integrated.

Risks to flag

- Technical: Integration of intermittent renewable sources may cause power quality issues; ensure UPS and battery systems are sized appropriately. - Compliance: UK GDPR requires data residency and processing transparency; verify that any new energy sourcing does not affect data center location or compliance posture. - Operational: Sudden shifts in energy supply can impact cooling efficiency; maintain robust monitoring and fail‑over plans. - Financial: Over‑investment in unproven turbine technology could lead to sunk costs; lock‑in PPAs with clear exit clauses.

Quick win

- Deploy a pilot energy monitoring dashboard in one region to track real‑time consumption and identify 5–10 % inefficiencies within 30 days. - Negotiate a short‑term renewable PPA or purchase of RECs to offset a portion of the current energy bill immediately.

Long-term play

- Over the next 6–12 months, develop a phased migration plan to replace a target percentage of data center power with certified renewable sources, leveraging existing utility incentives and carbon offset programs. - Invest in advanced cooling technologies (liquid cooling, free‑air cooling) to reduce overall energy demand, aligning with the broader industry shift toward higher PUE benchmarks.
